# The method in one sitting

Read this when you need to explain why a rule exists, or when the user asks what value
coding is. It is the book's argument, compressed, in the skill's own words. Credit the
ideas to Marco Yammine's book on value coding; where the book credits others (Levitt
on drills and holes, Ries on lean startups, Fitzpatrick on the mom test, Haidt on the
elephant and rider, Adzic on specification by example, Pareto on the 80/20), pass the
credit on.

## Value

Value is a real person's problem solved or a real person's opportunity created. Take
every human off the planet and a truck of gold is worth the same as a pile of dirt;
the only thing that makes anything valuable is the human on the other side. So the
question that is never wrong to ask, and that people will get annoyed at you for
asking, is "who is this helping?" Nobody needs AI, blockchain, an app, or a platform.
A new parent needs the diapers to be there. A landlord needs the midnight guest to get
the door code without calling. Tech is the vehicle, and code is the cheapest part.

Time is the only universal currency. Everyone has the same twenty-four hours, so
saving someone time is universally valuable, unless you shortened the part of their
day they wanted longer.

Value sometimes has to be taught. A child prefers candy to a banknote until someone
shows what the note does. If the person does not see the value yet, put the solution
aside, have the beer, and find out what sits on top of the problem you thought you
were solving.

## Why building alone feels good

The loop from idea to something that runs got very short, and with AI one person can
do it before lunch. Seeing the thing exist feels wonderful, and because it is so easy
to get there, people get a little addicted. Building is also where people hide: the
market might say no, and the feature cannot. Inside a company everything runs on
authority; outside it, with a customer, everything runs on consent, and you cannot
force a user to adopt anything. Roughly 80% of features shipped are never really
adopted. Nobody measures the equivalent number for initiatives and reorganisations,
which is how they get away with it.

AI made this worse and better at once. It is a guessing engine that produces something
that looks right, with total confidence, without ever having met the customer. It will
never ask who this is for. But it also made mistakes cheaper than ever: build for
thirty minutes, leave the house, talk to the person, come back. The gift is the
feedback loop, not the building, and only if the user leaves the house.

Be fast at trying and slow at committing. Ship to a real human in a week; spend a long
time watching what they do with it. Most founders do the reverse: commit in the
shower, iterate on features for a year, talk to nobody.

"Just the tip" is not market penetration. At least half the founders who say they
failed at go-to-market never fully entered any specific market; they got a reaction
they did not like and pulled out. The first sign of it not working is not the same as
it not working. Action is the antidote to anxiety.

## Virality, timing, and the second generation

Virality is not a strategy; it is the "build it and they will come" myth with a growth
curve drawn on it. A market does not penetrate itself. The companies that got a viral
boost and survived built sales teams and a decade of unglamorous retention afterwards.
Most profitable tech companies sell to businesses, and most consumer companies have a
business model underneath.

Timing predicts success better than the idea does, and timing is not luck; you can
only time a market you are standing in.

The generational problem: it is easy to gather the first wave around a cause and a
founder. The second generation, the people who arrived cold through a referral or an
ad, judge only on whether their problem gets solved, and they leave without saying
goodbye. Acquisition is the first generation; retention is the second; almost nobody
survives the second. The first thousand users are converts. A first month was never a
market.

## Selling

Salespeople hear "no" fifty times a day and are the only people in the building who
know what the market thinks. Everyone can build now, so building no longer makes
anyone stand out; engaging with humans does. The product never sells itself: somebody
wrote the page, chose the first sentence, and answered the fear before it was asked.
Money talks; a feature is a claim until someone pays for it.

## Emotion

People buy on emotion and justify with facts. The emotional brain drives; the rational
brain sits in the passenger seat listing what could go wrong, and left alone it never
leaves the parking lot. Spec sheets and comparison tables talk to the passenger. Lead
with the thing the person is afraid of or wants to be seen doing; give the facts
afterwards so they can defend the decision they already made. The same is true of the
builder: the roadmap is the rational brain writing down, afterwards, why the thing
that felt great to ship was never a risk. The only reliable difference between value
and self-gratification is whether someone else was in the room.

## The person, and the stranger

An ideal customer profile is a person you could pick out of a room, not a demographic.
The reason to be that specific is not that they are the only customer; it is that they
are the only customer you can talk to. Ten thousand people you understand beat ten
million you do not. Put the solution aside, have the beer, ask what is annoying, what
takes too long, what they do instead, and what they are embarrassed to admit. Use
their words back to them; it proves you listened.

Everyone who loves you will lie, not on purpose: they are protecting the relationship,
not evaluating the product. "I'd use that" is the most worthless sentence in the
language. Investors, advisors, and boards join that category the moment they are
invested. The only feedback that counts comes from people who do not care whether you
succeed, and the best feedback is their money. Ask for money before asking for
feedback; the feedback after "no, I won't pay" is the only kind worth writing down.

## The menu

The world's oldest profession worked out two thousand years ago that the offer has to
be visible before the sale, in a form a stranger can read at a glance. A deck, a home
page, and a first sentence on a call are doorways. Ten slides, one question each, in
order: person, outcome, way in, offer, timing, size, parties, competition, the dream,
the ask. Opening with the product before saying who it is for is the mistake nine in
ten make. See `menu.md`.

## Data

Building alone has no feedback loop, which is why it feels safe and never gets better.
Being data driven means watching what the other person actually does and adjusting,
not executing the plan harder. Numbers about you (sign-ups, views, downloads) tell you
the door works. Numbers about them (what they did next, where they stopped, what they
tried three times, what they did instead) tell you whether anyone wanted what was
behind it. Pick a number that is not the value and smart people will optimise it with
total commitment: the cobra bounty bred cobras. Feedback is information about the
thing; validation is information about you; they feel the same and do opposite jobs.
Point every piece of feedback at the intended value for the named person.

## The Raptor Method and altitudes

High, wide, patient; then narrow, fast, committed; then high again. Zoom out to see
the whole field, pick one target, dive with total focus, and whether you hit or miss,
climb back up. Finish work in progress or abandon it; if everything is priority one,
nothing is. A miss costs one dive; chasing the same mouse into the bushes for six
months costs the company. The Pi rule: three real attempts, then back to the perch.
Schedule the climb, because the details will never release you on their own.

Every conversation sits at an altitude: 40,000 ft (why this exists, who for), 30,000
(the market and the company in it), 20,000 (the product), 10,000 (the feature), ground
(the ticket, the button, and the humans, which is the only altitude where value
exists). Most arguments are two people at different altitudes. Now that teams are
three people or one, the same person climbs and dives all day, and the exhaustion is
the climbing, not the workload. See `altitudes.md`.

At 40,000 ft: roughly 80% of everything humans earn goes to staying alive, housed,
moving, connected, safe, and educated. Most startups fight over the other 20%. Boring
problems are where the money and the absence of competition are. Look for the field
with no other birds over it.

## Friction

The more objective friction between an idea and production, the higher its chance of
success. Bad friction is political and adds no information; remove it. Good friction
is a question that, once answered, makes the next decision better: who is this for,
what do they do instead, how do we know, what happens if we do not build it. The test:
after the friction, do you know something you did not? Without friction there is no
trust; the people you trust are the ones you have had it out with. Not yes-sayers,
not no-sayers: why-askers with an action bias. And every piece of friction has to come
with the empathy to carry it, or it wears people down instead of sharpening them.

## Pareto, nines, and never ready

Twenty percent of the effort produces eighty percent of the value; the next sixty
produces diminishing returns; the last twenty is a black hole. Everything created must
be maintained; every feature is a promise with rent attached. Nobody promises 100%:
the biggest companies on earth pick a number of nines and engineer to it, and each
extra nine costs ten times the last. There are two numbers of nines in every deal,
yours and the customer's, and founders usually carry a higher one than their customer
and build to it alone for months. When stakes do not allow 80%, the answer is not
courage; it is a team that has done it before, and still, rounds of 80%.

"Not ready" is a place to keep the excuse. Ship the 20%, embarrassed, and watch which
part people use. Building is not the enemy; building alone is. Keep the door open
while the work is ugly, because nobody leaves through a closed door. The failure mode
of the best engineers is the bodybuilder who forgot who the body was for: the gym
became the product and the mirror the customer.

## Risk, stakes, vulnerability, trust

Life is risky; get over it. Certainty does not exist and waiting for it is the most
expensive decision of all. Treat failure as a feature: make the risk visible, priced,
and deliberate. Most luck is buying many tickets with real odds and not stopping after
the ones that lost.

People with nothing to lose rarely achieve anything. Something has to be at stake that
cannot be taken back: money, a job left, a name told to people, six months of evenings.
Not the work; the stakes.

Trust is what turns a first time into a second, and it is built by friction, stakes,
vulnerability, and showing up again. You cannot ask a stranger to trust you with their
money until you have trusted them with the truth. When the fear stays a lion it is
because nobody opened the box: ask what the worst thing is, then "more specifically"
three times, and most lions turn out to be kittens. When it matters, call; when it
really matters, camera on.

## The sequence

Value coding is not a philosophy; it is a sequence. Name the person. Pick a market
small enough to reject you. Price it. Put a stake down. Meet strangers before writing
code, on video if not in person. If nobody commits on paper, on video, or with a
deposit, do not build it, and count that as the process working. Build the low-hanging
fruit only, zoom out every morning, ship the first release as a question, go back to
the strangers, iterate on what they do, pivot because you learned and not because you
got scared, reach paying customers who were never your friends, and watch who comes
back. The one number: of what was built, how much is still used and paid for a year
later, by name.

Do the right thing. Then do it right. But do it.
